# Pool & Aquatic Facilities

Drowning is fast and often silent. Pool operators prevent it through **supervision**, **rescue readiness**, **clear rules** and a **well-maintained facility**. This page is for anyone who owns or manages a pool or aquatic facility, including clubs, schools, hotels, condominiums and sports centres, and for organisers who hire one.

!!! warning "A pool licence is not a safety plan"
    A licence for an aquatic facility covers matters such as water quality and basic equipment: first-aid boxes, lifebuoys and displayed resuscitation instructions. **There is no general legal requirement for lifeguards** at licensed pools, so it is up to the operator to decide, through risk assessment, how the pool will be supervised and how a rescue will happen.

## Know the standards that apply

| Document | What it covers | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SS 700:2023** Code of Practice for Water Safety – Aquatic Facilities | Requirements and recommendations to prevent drowning and injury at aquatic facilities, including shallow water blackout and prolonged breath-holding | Current. Replaces TR 71:2019 |
| **SS 556:2021** Code of Practice for the Design and Management of Aquatic Facilities | Design, construction, equipment, operation, management and maintenance | Current |
| **SS 681:2022** Code of Practice for Sport Safety, clause 6 | Water safety principles for pools and open water | Current. Its reference to TR 71 should now be read as SS 700 |
| **Environmental Public Health (Licensable Aquatic Facilities) Regulations 2021** | Licensing of aquatic facilities and water quality, regulated by NEA | In force |

This page summarises key practices. To conform to a standard, obtain and apply the full document.

## Who is most at risk

Research on drowning in Singapore pools found that most incidents happened in **private pools without lifeguards**, such as condominium, hotel and home pools. Young children, weak or non-swimmers, people swimming alone, and people with medical conditions such as epilepsy or heart disease are at higher risk, as is anyone practising **breath-holding**.

## Lifeguard provision & competency

- Decide **whether lifeguards are needed, how many, and when**, through a risk assessment that considers pool size and depth, number and type of users, programmes running, opening hours, and features such as diving boards or slides.
- Where lifeguards are deployed, ensure they hold a **current lifesaving qualification** plus **CPR and AED certification**, and keep records of their training.
- Plan **rotations and breaks** so lifeguards stay alert, and keep them free of other duties while on watch.
- Where there are no lifeguards, put **other measures** in place: restricted hours, rules that children are supervised by an adult within arm's reach, CCTV or drowning-detection technology, clear emergency instructions, and staff trained in rescue and CPR who can respond quickly.

## Supervision & zoning

- Make sure the whole pool can be **seen** by whoever is supervising, with no blind spots from glare, structures or crowding.
- **Separate activities**: lane swimming, lessons, play and diving should not share the same water at the same time.
- Require **constant adult supervision** of infants, toddlers and pre-schoolers in and around water, even when they wear flotation aids. During lessons, parents should stay close and in direct sight of their children.
- Set and enforce an **age for unaccompanied use** and a maximum number of children per adult.

## Shallow water blackout & breath-holding

Prolonged or repeated **breath-holding**, especially after hyperventilating, can cause sudden loss of consciousness underwater without warning, even in strong swimmers and in shallow water.

- Prohibit **breath-holding competitions and games** and unsupervised hypoxic training.
- Brief coaches and lifeguards on the danger, and watch for swimmers who stay motionless underwater.
- Include the issue in user rules and signage.

## Rescue readiness & emergency action plan

- Provide **rescue equipment** where it can be reached quickly, such as rescue tubes, reaching poles and throw ropes, with instructions.
- Have an **AED** that can reach any casualty within **3 minutes**, in a visible, signed cabinet, plus a stocked **first-aid kit** and, where trained staff are present, **oxygen**.
- Keep **at least one staff member trained in CPR and AED** on duty whenever the facility is staffed, and refresh certification before it expires.
- Write an **emergency action plan**: who rescues, who calls 995, who fetches the AED, who clears the pool and who meets the ambulance. Include the address and access route. See [When to Call 995](../emergency/when-to-call-995.md).
- **Rehearse it at least twice a year**, including a simulated drowning rescue and CPR, and record the drills.

## Drowning prevention & signage

Display clear, visible rules and advice for swimmers:

- follow the safety rules and signs;
- never swim alone;
- learn swimming and water survival skills;
- understand the dangers of water;
- supervise children and weak swimmers at all times;
- swim only in safe, designated areas.

Also sign **depths**, **no-diving areas**, the **location of rescue equipment and the AED**, opening hours, and whether a lifeguard is on duty.

## Depth markings & diving

- Mark water **depth** clearly on the pool edge and walls, especially where the depth changes.
- **Prohibit diving** into shallow water. Diving into shallow water is a known cause of spinal injury.
- Allow diving only in areas with enough depth for the diving equipment provided, as set out in the design standard.

## Water quality & chemical handling

- Licensable aquatic facilities must meet the **water quality requirements** in the Environmental Public Health (Licensable Aquatic Facilities) Regulations, including testing by an accredited laboratory and submitting results to NEA at the required frequency.
- Store and handle **pool chemicals** such as chlorine-containing disinfectants safely: separate incompatible chemicals, train staff, provide personal protective equipment, and follow the joint government guidelines on the safe use and disposal of chlorine-containing chemicals.
- Assess chemical handling as part of your **WSH risk assessment** for staff and contractors.

## Programmes, hirers and events

- Require hirers, swim schools and event organisers to give you their **risk assessment and safety plan**, including how they will supervise and rescue, before they use the pool.
- Check that coaches and instructors are **registered** and hold current lifesaving and CPR/AED certification.
- For competitions and events, agree in writing who provides **lifeguards and medical cover**.

## Checklist: daily opening

- [ ] Water clear; bottom of the pool visible at the deep end
- [ ] Water test done and within limits
- [ ] Rescue equipment in place and serviceable
- [ ] AED present, indicator showing ready; first-aid kit stocked
- [ ] Lifeguard or trained staff on duty as planned
- [ ] Depth, no-diving and rules signage visible
- [ ] Pool surrounds free of slip and trip hazards; gates and barriers working
- [ ] Emergency phone and access route clear
