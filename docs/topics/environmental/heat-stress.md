# Heat Stress & Heat Illness

Singapore's heat and humidity make heat illness one of the most common serious risks in sport. Most cases can be prevented by adjusting activity to the conditions and to the people taking part. **Exertional heatstroke is a medical emergency: cool first, then transport.**

!!! danger "Someone confused, unsteady or collapsed while exercising in the heat?"
    Treat it as **exertional heatstroke**. Stop the activity, **call 995**, and **start cooling immediately**, ideally by immersing the body in cold or iced water up to the neck. [Jump to Respond](#respond)

## Check the heat stress level first

Singapore's national Heat Stress Advisory uses the **Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT)**, which combines air temperature, humidity, wind and sunshine into one measure of heat stress. Check it on the **myENV app** or **weather.gov.sg** for the sensor nearest your activity, before you start and during long sessions.

| Heat stress level | WBGT | General advice for prolonged outdoor activity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span class="ssp-chip ssp-chip--low">Low</span> | Below 31 °C | Continue normal activities. Stay hydrated. |
| <span class="ssp-chip ssp-chip--moderate">Moderate</span> | 31 °C to below 33 °C | **Reduce** outdoor activity. Take regular breaks in the shade or indoors, drink more fluids, wear light clothing, watch for signs of heat illness. |
| <span class="ssp-chip ssp-chip--high">High</span> | 33 °C and above | **Minimise** outdoor activity and stay in the shade where possible. Take more frequent and longer breaks, actively cool down (for example sponging or pouring water over arms and legs), watch for signs of heat illness. |

- **When a heatwave is declared**, organisers and facility operators should apply the High heat stress measures regardless of the local reading.
- WBGT is usually highest from **late morning to afternoon (about 11 am to 4 pm)**, and in the warmer months of April and May.
- The level describes the environment, not the person. The same WBGT is riskier for someone who is unfit, unacclimatised or unwell, or doing intense exercise in heavy clothing.

## Who is at higher risk

- People **exercising harder than their fitness allows**, including novices who over-pace themselves in races.
- People **not acclimatised** to Singapore's climate, such as visiting athletes or anyone returning after time in a cooler country.
- Anyone who has been **recently ill** (fever, sore throat, diarrhoea) or is still recovering.
- People taking **certain medicines**, such as stimulants, antihistamines or diuretics, which can reduce the body's ability to cope with heat.
- **Highly motivated athletes** who push through warning signs to perform.
- People with **higher body mass**, and **children and young people**, who may not judge the risk for themselves.
- Anyone wearing **heavy or protective clothing** or equipment.

## Prevent

**Build up gradually.** Acclimatise over about 10 to 14 days of gradually increasing exercise in the heat, supervised by a coach or with a partner. After a break from training, restart at a lower level than before.

**Do not exercise hard when unwell.** Skip intense training or competition with a fever or other signs of infection, and tell your coach.

**Drink to a plan, not to excess.** Losing up to 2 to 3% of body weight in sweat during exercise is normal for most people. Weigh yourself before and after training: each kilogram lost is about one litre of fluid. Use this to plan your drinking for races. Drinking far more than you lose can cause a dangerous drop in blood sodium (hyponatraemia).

**Adjust the activity to the level.** Shorten sessions, lower intensity, add rest breaks in shade, move to cooler times of day or indoors, and remove unnecessary clothing or equipment.

## Recognise

Heat illness ranges from cramps and heat exhaustion to **exertional heatstroke**, where body temperature rises above about 40.5 °C and the brain is affected. Heatstroke can kill or cause organ damage; the longer the body stays very hot, the worse the outcome.

=== "What the person may feel"

    - Dizziness or light-headedness
    - Headache
    - Nausea
    - Unusual tiredness, drowsiness or weakness
    - Cramps, numbness or weak arms and legs
    - Shortness of breath, even at rest

=== "What others may see"

    - **Confusion, disorientation, irritability or aggression**
    - Unsteady walking, stumbling or frequent falls
    - Rapid breathing
    - Vomiting
    - Seizures
    - Collapse or loss of consciousness

!!! warning "Confusion is the key warning sign"
    In someone exercising in the heat, any change in behaviour or mental state should be treated as exertional heatstroke until proven otherwise.

## Respond

### Suspected exertional heatstroke: an emergency

1. **Stop the activity** and move the person out of the sun if this is quick.
2. **Call 995.** See [When to Call 995](../../emergency/when-to-call-995.md).
3. **Cool immediately and aggressively.** Immerse the body (up to the neck, head supported) in **cold or iced water**: this is the most effective method and should be the default where it is available. If immersion is not possible, continuously apply ice-water-soaked towels over the whole body, rotating them, while fanning.
4. **Do not delay cooling** to wait for the ambulance or transport. Cool first, then transport, and keep cooling during transport where possible.
5. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, **start CPR and use an AED**.

### Heat exhaustion and milder symptoms

- Stop exercising and rest in the shade or a cool room.
- Remove extra clothing, cool the skin with water and fanning, and give fluids if the person can drink.
- **Call 995** if the person becomes confused, collapses, or does not improve quickly.
- Do not let them return to activity that day.

### Returning to activity

Return to training after heat illness should be decided case by case, with a doctor, based on how severe the episode was and the person's risk factors. After heatstroke, return should be gradual and medically supervised. See [Returning Safely](../../participants/returning-safely.md).

## What this means for you

=== "Participants"

    - Check the heat stress level before outdoor exercise and adjust what you do.
    - Build up gradually and do not train hard when unwell.
    - Know the warning signs and stop early. Tell someone if you feel unwell.
    - Look out for others: confusion in a team-mate or fellow runner means call 995 and cool.

=== "Coaches & exercise professionals"

    - Check WBGT before every outdoor session and keep checking during long sessions.
    - Plan acclimatisation for new or returning athletes and clients.
    - Ask about recent illness and medication before hard sessions.
    - Build in shade breaks and water access, and stop anyone showing warning signs.
    - Know how you would cool someone on the spot.

=== "Activity organisers"

    - Include heat in your [risk assessment](../../organisers/risk-assessment.md) and review it when the level is Moderate or higher: weather, participants' profile, intensity and extra controls.
    - Schedule outdoor activity outside the hottest hours where you can, and set decision points to shorten, modify, delay or cancel.
    - Provide shade, drinking water and rest points. For road races, plan water stations along the route (for marathons, about every 5 km).
    - Provide medical cover with **ice and cold-water immersion** for cooling, and an evacuation plan to the nearest hospital.
    - Brief participants, staff and volunteers on warning signs and how to raise the alarm.
    - Apply High measures when a heatwave is declared.

=== "Facility operators"

    - Provide shade, drinking water and cool rest areas, and signpost them.
    - Display how to check the heat stress level, and remind hirers to review their risk assessment.
    - Keep cooling equipment (ice, tubs or immersion facilities) and first aid ready where high-intensity activity takes place.
    - Protect your own outdoor staff by following Ministry of Manpower guidance on heat stress for outdoor work.
